Connectivity Modelling

To prioritize culverts for remediation, connectivity status and structure ranks were produced using BCFishPass, an open-source freshwater connectivity modelling framework, which builds off provincial barrier mapping tools (Mount et al. 2011) and includes three sub-models:

Accessibility model

Naturally accessible waterbodies are those that are considered likely accessible to focal species if no human-made barriers existed on the landscape. These were spatially delineated for each focal species using natural barriers (i.e., waterfalls, gradient barriers, or subsurface flows) that would naturally limit upstream movement.

Habitat model

A subset of the naturally accessible waterbody layer was defined as key habitat, i.e., habitat likely to support spawning or rearing, rather than simply being used as a movement corridor. The habitat model identifies areas that have a higher potential to support key habitat based on stream channel gradient and discharge.

Connectivity model

A layer of known or modelled structures was overlaid on the key habitat model output. Modelled stream crossings were created from stream network and road/railway Geographic Information System (GIS) data, by mapping where a road or railway crosses a stream. Dam data were obtained from the Canadian Aquatic Barriers Database. All mapped dams and modeled crossings (henceforth structures) downstream of key habitat were initially considered potential barriers.

Some structures were classified as non-existent or passable prior to initiating field work and excluded from the model. Stream crossings on 6th order and larger streams were assumed to be bridges and classified as passable. Remaining structures were checked on Google Earth to verify they were not bridges, cross-ditches, or non-existent crossings. Data from previous field assessments was obtained from the Provincial Stream Crossing Inventory System (PSCIS), and structures that had been assessed and scored as passable were classified as such. Assessed structures that were scored as barriers or potential barriers were considered barriers in the model, along with all dams lacking fishways and structures that have not yet been assessed. Models were run to produce initial outputs, and maps were created showing disconnected key habitat and associated structures.

Local knowledge and data were incorporated into model development in two phases and overruled modeled outputs where the two differed. Existing reports on habitat, barriers, and fish distributions, when available, were incorporated into the initial model outputs. Maps of initial outputs were then shared with local knowledge holders, who identified model errors or omissions (e.g., passable structures, or known spawning habitats that were not identified by the model). Corrections to model data were made based on local knowledge, and models were re-run.

Connectivity status was estimated by calculating the proportion of key habitat that is connected to the ocean (i.e., not fragmented by human-made barriers). Key habitat with no structures or only passable structures downstream was considered connected.

Ranking

Structures, and sets of structures that combine to disconnect habitat, were ranked by the amount of key habitat upstream. A ranked structure list helps determine where structures are blocking large amounts of habitat, and where removal of an individual structure or a group of structures (a “set”) is more effective in opening up habitat, by ranking structures based on amount of habitat blocked. Top-ranked structures or sets of structures represented those with the most key habitat upstream and did not consider other factors such as habitat quality and cost, which ultimately drove restoration prioritization when planning field work and deciding priorities with partners.

Habitat Accumulation Curves (HACs)

HACs are used to visualize structure rankings in order of the relative amount of habitat they are blocking upstream. Structures are plotted in order of km’s blocked by structures. HACs illustrate restoration priorities by showing how much habitat becomes accessible as barriers are removed or improved. This method of visualizing cumulative habitat gains is useful for prioritizing restoration work and communicating results to partners.

Structure Ranking

Lateral Barriers (Including to thermal refugia)

There is a lack of comprehensive data and mapping of lateral barriers and potential thermal refugia in the watershed to currently support a strategic prioritization (see Table 1). However, local knowledge was used to compile a list of candidate sites for field verification as a starting point to improve lateral and thermal connectivity. These sites were investigated in 2021 (Mozin (2021)), but an atmospheric river event later that winter resulted in major modifications and rearrangement of the Coldwater River and mainstem Nicola River downstream of the Coldwater confluence, rendering the outcome of those investigations no longer relevant. Further assessment of off-channel thermal refugia was undertaken by Nooaitch Band in 2023, with results of those assessments expected to be released to the broader community working on conservation in the Lower Nicola Watershed in the near future.
